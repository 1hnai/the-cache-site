Murakami started running in 1982 when he sold his jazz bar in Tokyo and committed to writing full-time. He has since completed more than thirty marathons and one ultramarathon. This book covers the year leading up to the 2005 New York City Marathon and the Athens-to-Athens ultramarathon, but it is not really about running.

What it is about: the daily discipline that sits behind any sustained creative work. Murakami writes at his desk by 4am, runs in the afternoon, and has maintained this rhythm for four decades. He is not romantic about it. Talent, he argues, is only part of the equation — focus and endurance are skills that can be trained, and running is how he trains them. "Pain is inevitable. Suffering is optional" is his distillation.

The prose is plain in the way that long-distance thinking tends to produce: stripped of ornamentation, honest about failure, precise about what actually works. He writes about aging, about the body's gradual refusal to do what you ask of it, and about the specific loneliness of any practice that is pursued alone over a long time.

It is a short book — under 200 pages — and belongs in the same category as Geoff Dyer's "Out of Sheer Rage" or John McPhee's "Draft No. 4": books about creative process that are interesting even if you have no interest in the ostensible subject.
