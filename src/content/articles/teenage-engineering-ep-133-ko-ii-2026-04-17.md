The original Pocket Operator PO-33 K.O! was the sampler that convinced a lot of people sampling didn't need to be complicated. The EP-133 takes that idea and scales it properly: more voices, a redesigned sequencer, song mode for chaining scenes together, and punch-in 2.0 effects that let you distort and mutilate sounds in real time without touching a menu.

The workflow is the point. Sample something - anything, from your phone speaker, from a record, from the room - and within minutes you're sequencing it across 16 steps, adding sidechain groove, layering it with something else. The song mode means you can arrange those patterns into something that feels like a finished piece without ever needing a computer. It records at 46.875kHz/16-bit stereo, which sounds technical but just means it captures sound accurately rather than as an affectation.

What Teenage Engineering understand, and what a lot of gear manufacturers miss, is that constraints are useful. The EP-133 runs on four AAA batteries. It has rubber keys rather than a pad matrix. The display is small. These aren't compromises - they're design decisions that focus what you can do and remove the paralysis that comes with too much choice.

At around £259, it's significantly cheaper than comparable hardware from Roland or Native Instruments, and considerably more interesting to use. If you make music - or want to start - this is the instrument that removes the excuses.
