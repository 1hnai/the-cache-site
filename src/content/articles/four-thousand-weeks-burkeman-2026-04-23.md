The self-help shelf is full of books that promise to make you more productive. Oliver Burkeman's *Four Thousand Weeks* does something more interesting: it argues that productivity, in its conventional sense, is the wrong frame entirely.

The maths in the title is blunt. A human lifespan of eighty years is roughly 4,000 weeks. That's it. The Anglo-American response to this scarcity has been to optimise — faster, more efficient, more done — but Burkeman's position, drawing from Heidegger and the Stoics, is that efficiency alone makes the problem worse. Every task you clear makes room for three more. The inbox empties for approximately six minutes before refilling.

What Burkeman offers instead is a philosophical reframe: accept finitude as the starting condition, not an obstacle to overcome. You will never do everything. The question shifts from "how do I fit it all in?" to "what am I willing to leave undone, and does what I'm actually doing reflect what matters to me?"

The writing is clear and unsentimentally warm. Burkeman spent years as a journalist covering self-help culture for *The Guardian*, so he's allergic to the genre's usual wishful thinking. The references are wide — Bertrand Russell, Marilynne Robinson, Japanese Zen — but worn lightly.

It doesn't leave you with a system. It leaves you with a harder question. For many readers, that turns out to be more useful.
