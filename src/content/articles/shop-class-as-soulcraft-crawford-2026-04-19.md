Matthew Crawford has a PhD in political philosophy from the University of Chicago. He left a think tank job to open a motorcycle repair shop in Richmond, Virginia. *Shop Class as Soulcraft* is his attempt to explain why that decision made intellectual sense.

The argument is not anti-intellectual. It's the opposite. Crawford's central claim is that skilled manual work — the kind that requires diagnosing a specific problem, developing a fix, and testing whether it actually works — demands a form of engaged thinking that white-collar knowledge work often forecloses. The motorcycle engine is not a metaphor. It's a genuinely complex system that doesn't care about your credentials or your ability to write persuasive memos. Either the bike runs or it doesn't.

The book draws on philosophy (Aristotle, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty), economics, and Crawford's own experience to examine what gets lost when we sever thinking from making. Published in 2009, it's grown more relevant as the economy has drifted further toward abstraction and remote work has made the intangibility of most professional labour even more pronounced.

It's also well-written. Crawford has a dry, precise style that sits comfortably between academic rigour and personal essay. The passages about learning to ride and repair motorcycles are genuinely pleasurable, and the philosophical sections are lucid rather than laboured.

If you've ever found yourself preferring the concrete satisfaction of fixing something — a bike, a piece of furniture, a leaking tap — to the ambient anxiety of managing open tasks and unresolved emails, this book will put words to the feeling with unusual clarity.
