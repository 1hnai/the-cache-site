Norwegian Wood became a phenomenon in Scandinavia before it was translated into English, which is itself a remarkable thing for a book about firewood. Lars Mytting wrote it as a practical guide - how to identify timber, when to fell, how to split and stack, how to dry and season, and how to get the best burn from a wood-fired stove. It covers all of that with authority.

But it is also something else. The book is saturated with a specific kind of Nordic patience - the idea that good preparation done months in advance produces results that shortcuts cannot. There are interviews with elderly Norwegians about their stacking methods. There are chapters on the philosophy of living in cold climates. There is a dry humour throughout that keeps the writing alive across technical subject matter.

Reading Norwegian Wood changes how you look at a stack of firewood. What was once background material becomes something with an internal logic: whether the grain runs straight, how the bark cracks, the weight distribution across a cord. Mytting has found in a very old human activity a way to talk about craft, preparation, and the satisfaction of work that pays off slowly.

At £12 this is among the better reading investments available. It sits comfortably alongside books like The Craftsman or Pirsig's Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance in its ability to find depth in the practical.
